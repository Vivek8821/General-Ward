# Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 — Compliance Guide for General Ward Patient Management Software

---

## Part 1: Legislative Status and Implementation Timeline

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 received the assent of the President on 11 August 2023. [DPO India](https://www.dpdpact2023.com/) However, the Act alone did not immediately create enforceable obligations. On November 13, 2025, the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) notified the DPDP Act and the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025. [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/?t=law&c=IN)

The Central Government has adopted a deliberate, staggered approach to commencing the DPDP Act and the DPDP Rules 2025, ensuring the enforcement structure is ready before the core compliance obligations are activated. The provisions that commenced immediately focus on establishing the institutional machinery and laying down the foundational legal definitions. [Taxmann](https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/analysis-indias-dpdp-act-and-rules)

The three-phase implementation schedule is as follows.

Phase I, which is effective immediately, covers provisions in relation to setting up the Data Protection Board of India. Phase III, effective 18 months from notification (that is, from 13 May 2027), covers all other substantive provisions of the DPDP Act, including specific compliance obligations. Essentially, entities have 18 months to prepare for the implementation of the DPDP Act. [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/?t=law&c=IN)

The practical implication for this software is that while full enforcement of compliance obligations does not activate until May 2027, the architecture and workflow decisions being made in development today will determine whether the software is compliant when that deadline arrives. Building compliance in now is significantly less costly than retrofitting it under regulatory pressure in 2027.

## Part 2: A Critical Distinction — No Special Category for Health Data

This is the most important structural difference between the DPDPA 2023 and comparable frameworks such as the GDPR, and it has direct implications for how this software must be designed.

Notably, and in contrast to other international data protection frameworks, the DPDPA treats all personal data uniformly, without imposing heightened obligations for sensitive personal data. [Hogan Lovells](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/indias-digital-personal-data-protection-act-2023-brought-into-force-)

This means health data — patient diagnoses, medication records, vitals, clinical notes — carries no additional statutory obligations beyond what applies to any other personal data under the Act. There is no concept of a "special category" that attracts extra requirements the way GDPR Article 9 does for health data. Every obligation described in this document applies uniformly to all personal data the hospital processes, including clinical records.

## Part 3: Who Is Who — Legal Roles Under the Act

Identifying the correct legal role for each party is essential before mapping compliance obligations, because the Act assigns duties differently depending on role.

The hospital is the **Data Fiduciary** — any person who, alone or with others, determines the purpose and means of processing personal data. [Hogan Lovells](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/indias-digital-personal-data-protection-act-2023-brought-into-force-) The hospital decides why patient data is collected, what is recorded, and for how long it is retained. The hospital bears the primary compliance burden under the Act.

Your software company, under the on-premise model, is in a limited Data Processor position. The DPDP Act clearly states that the Data Fiduciary remains primarily and ultimately responsible for compliance (Section 8(1)), regardless of any processing carried out by a Data Processor. [Taxmann](https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/analysis-indias-dpdp-act-and-rules) Because the hospital operates the software on its own server without your company having ongoing access to the data, the hospital carries full responsibility.

The patient is the **Data Principal** — the individual whose personal data is being processed and whose rights the Act protects.

## Part 4: Grounds for Processing Patient Data

Personal data may be processed only for a lawful purpose upon consent of an individual. Consent may not be required for specified legitimate uses such as voluntary sharing of data by the individual or processing by the State for permits, licenses, benefits, and services. [PRSIndia](https://prsindia.org/billtrack/digital-personal-data-protection-bill-2023)

For a hospital general ward, the primary basis for processing patient data is clinical care delivery, which falls within the Act's legitimate uses provisions. Consent is not needed where the processing relates to essential services such as healthcare. [Pib](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc20251117695301.pdf) This is a practical and important provision — it means emergency admissions and acute care do not require a formal consent workflow before treatment can begin or clinical data can be recorded. The hospital is not legally required to obtain digital consent before recording a patient's vitals or administering medication.

However, this exemption applies to processing necessary for healthcare delivery. Any secondary use of patient data — analytics, research, reporting to third parties, performance benchmarking — requires a separate lawful basis, which in most cases will be explicit consent.

---

## Part 5: Notice Obligations (Section 5, Rule 3)

Even where consent is not the operative basis for processing, the Act imposes a notice obligation. Before collecting personal data, the Data Fiduciary must provide the patient with a notice covering what data is being collected, the purpose of processing, and how the patient may exercise their rights.

Rule 3 specifies the required format for the accompanying notice — it must be presented clearly and be understandable independently of any other information provided. The notice must include an itemised description of the personal data sought, the specified purpose of processing, and a specific description of the goods or services to be provided. Additionally, the notice must outline the means by which the Data Principal can exercise their rights, including the right to withdraw consent. [Taxmann](https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/analysis-indias-dpdp-act-and-rules)

The current software does not generate or manage such a notice at the point of patient admission. The admission form captures clinical and demographic data but has no structured notice workflow. This is a compliance gap that requires a dedicated admission notice feature — or at minimum, a field on the patient record confirming that notice was provided, by whom, and when.

---

## Part 6: Consent Standards (Section 6)

Where consent is required — for secondary uses of patient data — the DPDP Act requires a high standard for valid consent (Section 6), which must be "free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous with a clear affirmative action." [Taxmann](https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/analysis-indias-dpdp-act-and-rules)

Every request for consent under the provisions of this Act shall be presented to the Data Principal in a clear and plain language, giving her the option to access such request in English or any language specified in the Eighth Schedule to the Constitution. [PRSIndia](https://prsindia.org/files/bills_acts/bills_parliament/2023/Digital%20Personal%20Data%20Protection%20Bill,%202023.pdf)

Rule 3(c)(i) explicitly mandates that the ease of withdrawing consent must be comparable to the ease with which consent was initially given. This anti-dark pattern provision imposes a clear technical requirement — if consent is one-click, withdrawal must be similarly straightforward, backed by audit trails to demonstrate compliance. [Taxmann](https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/analysis-indias-dpdp-act-and-rules)

In practical terms for this software, if the hospital ever uses patient data for a purpose beyond direct care delivery — such as sharing anonymised records with a research institution — the system must present a plain-language consent screen in the patient's preferred language, and the patient must be able to withdraw that consent through an equally simple mechanism.

## Part 7: Rights of the Data Principal (Sections 11–14)

The Act confers four enforceable rights on patients. The hospital must be capable of operationally satisfying each of them, which means the software must provide the administrative tools to do so.

**Right to Access Information (Section 11).** A patient is entitled to obtain a summary of the personal data the hospital holds about them and information about who that data has been shared with. The Patient Treatment Report introduced in Phase 11 substantially satisfies this right for clinical data. What is not currently captured is a log of any data sharing — if patient data is transmitted to another system or referral institution, that transmission must be recorded and disclosable on request.

**Right to Correction and Erasure (Section 12).** Citizens have the right to access their data, request its correction within a 90-day window, and demand erasure when the stated purpose of processing is fulfilled. [Scrut](https://www.scrut.io/post/dpdp-rules) The software allows editing of patient demographic data but does not currently maintain a structured log of formal correction requests, their review, and their resolution. An administrative correction request workflow is a compliance requirement.

**Right to Grievance Redressal (Section 13).** The hospital must establish a mechanism for receiving and resolving patient complaints about data handling. The software does not currently include a grievance tracking register or a designated Data Protection Officer workflow. At minimum, the system needs a grievance log — a simple administrative module where complaints are recorded, assigned, and marked resolved with the resolution documented.

**Right to Nominate (Section 14).** A patient may nominate another individual to exercise their data rights on their behalf in the event of death or incapacity. The software captures an emergency contact field on the patient record. This field needs to be formally linked to the nomination framework so that it is treated as a data rights nomination, not merely a clinical contact.

## Part 8: Data Fiduciary Obligations (Section 8)

Beyond rights-correlative obligations, the Act imposes affirmative duties on hospitals as Data Fiduciaries.

**Data accuracy.** The hospital must take reasonable steps to ensure that personal data is accurate and complete. The current validation logic on data entry partially satisfies this. A systematic mechanism for flagging incomplete mandatory fields across active patient records would strengthen compliance.

**Security safeguards (Rule 6).** The Act and Rules require Data Fiduciaries to implement reasonable security safeguards to prevent data breaches. Businesses must use encryption, access control, and other security measures to protect personal data. Regular security audits should be conducted to identify vulnerabilities and rectify them. [Strobes](https://strobes.co/blog/dpdp-rules-2025-what-everything-you-need-to-know/) The specific technical standards are not prescribed in the Act itself and are left to the rules — however, the Rules confirm that safeguards must include access controls, encryption, and audit logging. For this software, the absence of encryption at rest for the database is the most material gap, addressed by the PostgreSQL migration combined with LUKS disk encryption on the server.

**System log retention (Rule 6).** Data Fiduciaries must retain system and processing logs and associated traffic data for a minimum of one year for detection, investigation, and remediation purposes. [Scrut](https://www.scrut.io/post/dpdp-rules) The current audit log is retained indefinitely, which satisfies the minimum — but there is no formal policy document specifying the retention period, which would be required in a compliance audit.

**Storage limitation and data erasure (Rule 8).** Personal data cannot be stored beyond one year of user inactivity, unless legally required. Users must receive a 48-hour advance notice before their data is erased due to inactivity. [India Briefing](https://www.india-briefing.com/news/dpdp-rules-2025-india-data-protection-law-compliance-40769.html/) For a hospital software context, medical records are subject to the National Medical Commission's retention guidelines (which superseded the older MCI framework after 2020), specifying minimum retention periods that vary by record type. The DPDPA storage limitation does not override these retention obligations — legally mandated retention constitutes a valid ground for retaining data beyond the inactivity threshold. However, the software currently has no retention policy engine at all. Records are held indefinitely with no automated review, no inactivity tracking, and no erasure workflow. This requires a configurable retention management system.

## Part 9: Breach Notification Obligations

In the event of a data breach, businesses must notify both the affected individuals and the Data Protection Board of India promptly. [Strobes](https://strobes.co/blog/dpdp-rules-2025-what-everything-you-need-to-know/) The DPDP Rules 2025 operationalise this obligation with specific procedural requirements. The notification must describe the nature of the breach, the categories of data affected, the approximate number of individuals affected, and the measures being taken to address it.

The current software does not include a breach detection or notification workflow. The audit log contains the data necessary to investigate a breach — it records all mutations by user, timestamp, and action. What is missing is an administrative interface that converts this data into a formal breach notification report: which patient records were accessed or modified during a given window, by which user accounts, and what data categories were involved. This is a defined development task that does not require architectural changes — it is a reporting interface built on top of the existing audit log.

## Part 10: Children's Data (Section 9, Rule 12)

The bill requires all data fiduciaries to obtain verifiable consent from the legal guardian before processing the personal data of [PRSIndia](https://prsindia.org/billtrack/digital-personal-data-protection-bill-2023) a child, defined as a person under 18 years of age.

The DPDP Rules 2025 acknowledge this sensitivity, particularly around children's data, while mandating a comprehensive compliance baseline for every healthcare entity. One of the most healthcare-relevant features of the DPDP Rules lies in Rule 12, which grants conditional exemptions for processing a child's personal data. This recognises real-world clinical situations where care cannot be delayed by administrative consent cycles, protecting hospitals, paediatric units, and emergency rooms from legal friction. However, these exemptions do not dilute the overall security, transparency, or breach-notification obligations that still apply. [eHEALTH](https://ehealth.eletsonline.com/2025/11/the-healthcare-centric-guide-to-dpdp-rules-2025-what-indias-healthcare-providers-companies-must-know/)

The practical implication is that when a patient is under 18, the system must flag the record as a minor, capture the identity of the parent or guardian, and record that the guardian was given notice at the time of admission. The current patient record does not distinguish minors in a way that triggers a different compliance workflow. This is a specific feature gap.

## Part 11: Significant Data Fiduciaries (Section 10, Rule 13)

The DPDP Act empowers the Government to designate any data fiduciary or class of data fiduciaries as a "Significant Data Fiduciary" (SDF), using a series of criteria that include volume and sensitivity of personal data processed and the risk posed to the rights of data principals, as well as broader societal concerns including potential impact on the sovereignty and integrity of India. [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/the-digital-personal-data-protection-act-of-india-explained/)

A single Tier 2 hospital operating one installation of this software is unlikely to meet the threshold for SDF designation. However, a hospital chain deploying the software across multiple facilities and aggregating patient data could reach that threshold. SDFs face additional obligations including annual Data Protection Impact Assessments and independent data protection audits. Every SDF must conduct a comprehensive DPIA and an independent data protection audit at least once every 12 months, with the findings reported to the Data Protection Board. [India Briefing](https://www.india-briefing.com/news/dpdp-rules-2025-india-data-protection-law-compliance-40769.html/)

This is noted for future reference. If the software is adopted by a hospital group rather than individual facilities, revisiting SDF obligations becomes necessary.

---

## Part 12: Cross-Border Data Transfers (Section 16)

The DPDP Act does not currently restrict the transfer of personal data outside of India. It reverses the typical paradigm of international data transfer provisions by presuming that transfers may occur without restrictions, unless the Government specifically restricts transfers to certain countries. [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/the-digital-personal-data-protection-act-of-india-explained/)

Under the on-premise model, where the hospital's data never leaves their own server, cross-border transfer provisions have no practical relevance. This changes if the software is ever offered as a hosted or cloud service, or if any third-party integrations (such as cloud backup services) are added.

## Part 13: Penalties (Schedule to the Act)

The penalty figures are set in the Schedule to the DPDPA 2023 and remain as stated in the earlier analysis. Failure to implement reasonable security safeguards resulting in a breach carries a penalty of up to ₹250 crore. Failure to notify the Data Protection Board of a breach carries a penalty of up to ₹200 crore. Violations related to children's data carry a penalty of up to ₹200 crore. Non-compliance with Significant Data Fiduciary obligations carries a penalty of up to ₹150 crore. All other violations carry a penalty of up to ₹50 crore.

It is important to note that these are maximum fine limits, not fixed fines. The Data Protection Board of India will determine the actual penalty amount only after conducting an official investigation, considering several factors before deciding on a penalty. [Prophaze](https://www.prophaze.com/dpdp-act-2025-rules-compliance-penalties/)

## Part 15: Compliance Timeline Relative to Your Go-to-Market

The DPDP Act and DPDP Rules are being implemented in a phased manner. All other substantive provisions, including specific compliance obligations, are effective 18 months from the date of notification, that is, from May 13, 2027. [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/?t=law&c=IN)

Your target go-to-market window precedes this enforcement date. This means the hospital software you are selling can legally begin operating before the full compliance regime is enforced. However, selling software that enables hospitals to begin accumulating patient data without a compliance pathway in place exposes those hospitals to retrofitting costs and regulatory risk as May 2027 approaches. The commercially sound position is to build compliance features into the product before the first hospital goes live, so that every customer is compliant from day one rather than scrambling to catch up eighteen months later. This is also a sales differentiator — hospitals procuring software in 2026 will increasingly assess DPDPA readiness as a vendor selection criterion.
